# Day 1: Traffic patterns and the three-tier design

## 1. What a data center network must do

A data center network connects thousands of servers and storage systems to **each other** and to the **outside world**. Compared with an office (campus) network, it needs:

- **Bandwidth:** servers push 10, 25 or 100 Gbps each.
- **Predictable, low latency:** applications are sensitive to delay and jitter.
- **Resilience:** any single link or switch can fail with no outage.
- **Scale:** you can grow from 10 racks to 100 without redesigning.
- **Automation:** hundreds of identical switches must be configured consistently, which is where groups and, later, Apstra come in.

## 2. North-south vs east-west traffic

| | North-south | East-west |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Direction | In and out of the data center: users or the internet ↔ servers | Server ↔ server **inside** the data center |
| Examples | A user loading a web page | Web server calling an app server, then a database; storage replication; VM migration; AI training jobs syncing GPUs |
| Share today | Minority | **Majority** of traffic in modern data centers |

Older networks were designed for north-south traffic. Modern applications are split into many services that constantly talk to each other, so **east-west traffic dominates**. That single shift drives most of this week's design changes.

## 3. The traditional three-tier design

Here is the classic design, with the problems marked:


**The three tiers:**

| Tier | Role |
| --- | --- |
| **Access** | Where servers plug in. Pure Layer 2: VLANs, no routing. |
| **Aggregation** (distribution) | Collects the access switches. The **Layer 2/Layer 3 boundary**: the server default gateways live here, usually as a redundant pair using VRRP. Firewalls and load balancers often attach here. |
| **Core** | Fast Layer 3 backbone connecting aggregation blocks and the exits to campus, WAN and internet. |

VLANs usually stretch from the access layer up to the aggregation pair, so the whole lower half of the design is one large Layer 2 network.

## 4. Why this design struggles today

**1. Spanning Tree Protocol (STP) wastes half the links.** Every access switch connects to both aggregation switches for redundancy, but in Layer 2 that creates a **loop**. A frame could circle forever, multiplying until the network melts down in a **broadcast storm**. STP prevents this by **blocking** redundant links (the dashed lines in the diagram). The backup links sit idle until a failure, so you pay for bandwidth you can't use.

**2. STP is slow and fragile.** When a link fails, STP takes from a second up to about 50 seconds (classic STP) to re-converge, and traffic drops meanwhile. A misconfiguration or faulty device can cause a loop that takes down the entire Layer 2 domain.

**3. East-west traffic takes the long way.** Look at Server A to Server B. They're on different access switches, so traffic climbs to Agg 1 and comes back down. If they're in different VLANs, it must also be routed at the gateway. Path length varies with where the servers sit, so latency is unpredictable, and the aggregation pair carries almost all server-to-server traffic.

**4. Large failure domains.** One Layer 2 domain spans many racks. A broadcast storm, MAC table overflow or STP bug affects all of them.

**5. Limited scale.** Growth is capped by how many ports and how much capacity the aggregation pair has. Adding more means building a whole new "pod" and connecting pods through the core. That's another tier, more hops and more bottlenecks.

**6. Compounding oversubscription** (next section).

## 5. Oversubscription

**Oversubscription** is the ratio of bandwidth *into* a switch from below to bandwidth *out* of it toward the next tier:

> **Oversubscription = total downstream (server-facing) bandwidth ÷ total upstream (uplink) bandwidth**

**Example:** an access switch with 48 × 10G server ports = 480 Gbps down, and 4 × 40G uplinks = 160 Gbps up. 480 ÷ 160 = **3:1**. If every server sent at full speed toward the uplinks, only a third would fit.

Some oversubscription is normal, because servers rarely all transmit at once. The problem in three tiers is that **it multiplies at each tier**: 3:1 at access, plus another 4:1 at aggregation, can give an effective 12:1 for traffic crossing the core. And with STP blocking half the uplinks, the real figure is worse still.

Typical modern targets are 3:1 or better for general workloads, and **1:1 (non-blocking)** for storage and AI/GPU fabrics.

## 6. How the industry, and Juniper, patched it

Before moving fully to IP fabrics, vendors tried to make Layer 2 less painful:

| Approach | Idea | You'll meet it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Virtual Chassis** (Juniper) | Several switches act as one logical switch, with one config and no STP between members | Week 9 (JNCIA-DC lists it under high availability) |
| **MC-LAG** | Two switches each terminate one side of a LAG, so a server or switch uses both links without STP blocking | Week 3, Day 3; JNCIP-DC |
| **Virtual Chassis Fabric / QFabric** (Juniper) | Larger "one big switch" designs | Historical context |

These reduce STP pain, but they keep large Layer 2 domains and proprietary scaling limits. The lasting answer is the **spine-leaf IP fabric**, which is tomorrow's topic.

## Question 1: Which links does STP block?

**Setup:** 8 access switches (A1–A8), each dual-homed to Agg 1 and Agg 2. The two aggregation switches are linked to each other, and each connects to both core switches.

**With Agg 1 as root bridge:**

| Link | State | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A1–A8 → Agg 1 (8 links) | **Forwarding** | The direct path to the root, so it becomes each access switch's root port |
| A1–A8 → Agg 2 (8 links) | **Blocked** (8 links) | A second path to the root; keeping it open would create a loop |
| Agg 1 ↔ Agg 2 | **Forwarding** | Agg 2's best path to the root |
| Aggregation ↔ core (4 links) | **Not in STP** | These are Layer 3 routed links; STP only runs inside the Layer 2 domain |

So **8 of 16 access uplinks (50%) carry no traffic** until a failure.

**A detail that shows up in exams:** a blocked link is blocked at **one end**, not both. On each A↔Agg 2 link, the port on the **access switch** is the one blocked. Agg 2 is normally configured as the *secondary root*, with a better (lower) bridge priority than the access switches, so its port wins the "designated" role and the access switch's port becomes the alternate (blocked) port.

## Question 2: How many hops between servers?

Server A is on Access 1 and Server B on Access 5.

| Case | Path | Switch hops |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Same VLAN | Access 1 → Agg 1 → Access 5 | **3** |
| Different VLANs, gateway (VRRP master) on Agg 1 | Access 1 → Agg 1 (routed here) → Access 5 | **3**, including one routing step |
| Different VLANs, but VRRP master on **Agg 2** while STP root is on Agg 1 | Access 1 → Agg 1 → Agg 2 (routed) → Agg 1 → Access 5 | **5** |
| Both servers on the **same** access switch, same VLAN | Access 1 only | **1** |

Two lessons:
- **Hop count depends on where the servers sit,** which is why latency is unpredictable in three-tier designs. A spine-leaf fabric makes it the same for every pair (tomorrow).
- **The 5-hop case is a classic real-world mistake.** If the STP root and the VRRP master are on different aggregation switches, traffic crosses the Agg 1 ↔ Agg 2 link unnecessarily. Best practice is to make the same switch both STP root and VRRP master for each VLAN.

That row needs three ideas you haven't covered yet: subnets per VLAN, default gateways and VRRP. Here they are from the beginning, using the same picture. This is background for week 3; VRRP itself isn't a JNCIA-DC exam topic.

## 1. Different VLANs need a router between them

Each VLAN is a separate network with its own IP subnet:

| | VLAN | Subnet | Server IP |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Server A | VLAN 10 | 192.168.10.0/24 | 192.168.10.5 |
| Server B | VLAN 20 | 192.168.20.0/24 | 192.168.20.5 |

Server A can't reach Server B directly, because they're in different subnets. It must send the packet to its **default gateway**, a router interface in its own subnet, for example 192.168.10.1. The gateway **routes** the packet into VLAN 20 and delivers it to Server B. That's the "routing step" in the table.

In a three-tier design, the gateway lives on the **aggregation switches**.

## 2. VRRP: two switches sharing one gateway

You have two aggregation switches, so the gateway shouldn't depend on just one of them. **VRRP** (Virtual Router Redundancy Protocol) lets them share a gateway:

- Both switches agree on one **virtual gateway IP**, 192.168.10.1, which is what the servers use.
- At any moment, only **one** switch, the **master**, actually answers for that IP and routes the traffic.
- The other is the **backup**. If the master fails, the backup takes over the same IP within about a second, and the servers never notice.

## 3. STP decides which way frames travel

Separately, STP (from Day 1) decides the **Layer 2 path**. With Agg 1 as root, every access switch's link to **Agg 2 is blocked**. So from any access switch, the only open road leads to **Agg 1**.

## 4. Putting it together

**Case 1: the master is on Agg 1** (STP root and VRRP master on the same switch)
1. Server A sends to its gateway. The frame travels up the only open road: Access 1 → **Agg 1**.
2. Agg 1 *is* the active gateway, so it routes the packet into VLAN 20.
3. It sends it down the open road: Agg 1 → Access 5 → Server B.

**3 switches: Access 1, Agg 1, Access 5.** ✅

**Case 2: the master is on Agg 2** (STP root on Agg 1, VRRP master on Agg 2)
1. Server A sends to its gateway. The frame still travels up the only open road: Access 1 → **Agg 1**.
2. Agg 1 isn't the active gateway, so it just switches the frame along the Agg 1 ↔ Agg 2 link to **Agg 2**.
3. Agg 2 routes the packet into VLAN 20.
4. Agg 2's link to Access 5 is **blocked**, so it must send the packet back over the Agg 2 ↔ Agg 1 link to **Agg 1**.
5. Agg 1 sends it down: Access 1... no, Access 5 → Server B.

**5 switch visits: Access 1, Agg 1, Agg 2, Agg 1, Access 5.** ❌ The traffic crosses the Agg 1 ↔ Agg 2 link twice for no reason.

**An analogy:** the post office (gateway) is in Town 2, but the only open road from your village (STP) goes to Town 1. Every letter goes to Town 1, across to Town 2 to be sorted, then back through Town 1 to reach the other village.

## The takeaway

When **one switch is both the STP root and the VRRP master**, the open roads lead straight to the active gateway, and traffic takes the short path. When they're split, traffic zig-zags between the aggregation switches, wasting bandwidth and adding delay.

This is one more reason three-tier designs are fiddly: you have to keep two separate protocols aligned by hand. In the spine-leaf designs coming next (and EVPN-VXLAN later), every leaf acts as the gateway itself, so this problem disappears.

## Question 4: Your lab as a three-tier design

**Where SW1 and SW2 fit:** they're the **aggregation pair**. They're linked to each other, servers (H1, H2) hang off them, and in week 5 you'll configure the **IRB gateways** on them, which is exactly the aggregation role. Lab A has no separate access tier; the hosts connect straight to aggregation, and there's no core.

**What breaks first if you add 10 more switches** as access switches, each dual-homed to SW1 and SW2:

1. **STP blocks half the new uplinks.** 10 of the 20 new links sit idle immediately.
2. **SW1 and SW2 become the bottleneck.** All east-west traffic between the new racks passes through them, limited by their ports and capacity.
3. **The Layer 2 failure domain grows.** All 12 switches share one Layer 2 network, so one loop or broadcast storm affects every rack.

The first problem appears the moment you cable them. The second shows up as traffic grows. The third only hurts when something goes wrong, but then it hurts everything at once.

Tomorrow's spine-leaf design is built to remove all three.
